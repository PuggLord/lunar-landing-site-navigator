When the landing date moves, the sunlight and the radio link move with it.

At the Moon's south pole the Sun and the Earth both stay within a few degrees of the horizon, and that horizon is ridges and crater rims. A new date, or a site a little closer to the pole, can cost a lander its power or its link to Earth for days.

The data to check this is public, in JPL Horizons, Quickmap and Moon Trek. The challenge statement names the gap: judging each site and date means moving between several interfaces and reading raw numbers.

A planner with two options has to run that twice and work out the difference by hand.

Left, the IM-1 landing site: the Earth stays up for all 90 days. Right, the planned IM-2 site: the Earth sets after 9 days, and the longest break in the radio link lasts 5 d 17 h. That site gets more sunlight, 1,389 against 1,164 kWh per m². The grey ground in each chart is the real skyline.

Lunar Landing Site Navigator4 / 7

Step off the rim of Shackleton and the Sun never rises.

Two places 10 km apart at the south pole, over 90 days. The rim has sunlight 91% of the time. The floor has none.

The horizon in the tool is the ground itself: NASA LOLA laser altimeter heights on a 240 m grid, read in 720 compass directions out to 200 km.

With a flat horizon the tool gives 53% and 47%, and the difference between the two places disappears.

Every number has a check behind it.

NASA data in the tool. LOLA polar elevation grids at 240 m for the skyline. JPL Horizons for the reference tables. Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter colour mosaic for the 3D Moon. Site coordinates from NASA, LROC, NSSDCA and USGS pages, each linked on the page.

What it leaves out. Terrain is used within 17° of a pole and the horizon is flat elsewhere. Solar panel angle, lander tilt and antenna pointing are not modelled. The page says so beside the numbers.

How it is built

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. It opens from a file with no network and no account. Astronomy Engine (MIT) gives the positions and NASA LOLA terrain gives the skyline. The 3D Moon is our own WebGL code.

Use of AI

The idea is the team's. The code, the tests and these slides were written with Claude Code under the team's direction. Every file is labelled and SI_USE.md logs each session. Nothing here is an AI-generated image.
