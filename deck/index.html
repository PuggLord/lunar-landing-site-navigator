Lunar Landing Site Navigator

Compare two landing sites or two landing dates on the Moon, and see which one loses the Sun or the Earth first.

Moon imagery: NASA Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, drawn by our code

1 / 7

A two-week slip can leave a polar lander in the dark.

At the Moon's south pole the Sun and the Earth both sit within a few degrees of the horizon, and the horizon there is ridges and crater rims. Change the date, or move the site closer to the pole, and a lander can lose power or its link to Earth for days.

The data needed to check this is public, in JPL Horizons, Quickmap and Moon Trek. The challenge statement points out the catch: checking one site and date means going between several interfaces and reading raw numbers.

With two options on the table, a planner does that twice and then works out the difference by hand.

Lunar Landing Site Navigator2 / 7

Pick two scenarios and read the answer.

  • The first thing on screen is the answer. This is the IM-1 site on its landing day and 14 days later. The late lander waits 12 d 2 h in the dark for sunrise.
  • Choose from six landing sites with sourced coordinates, or click the Moon to place your own.
  • Press Play to run the period forward. The day and night line moves across the Moon and the charts keep pace.
Lunar Landing Site Navigator3 / 7

Move the site 175 km and the Earth starts to set.

On the left is the IM-1 landing site, where the Earth stays up for all 90 days. At the planned IM-2 site on the right it sets after 9 days, and the longest gap is 5 d 17 h. That site collects more sunlight, 1,389 against 1,164 kWh per m². The grey ground is the real skyline.

Lunar Landing Site Navigator4 / 7

At Shackleton crater, 10 km separates 91% sunlight from none.

These are two points at the south pole over 90 days. The one on the rim is sunlit 91% of the time, and the one on the floor gets no sunlight.

The tool gets this right because its horizon is the ground. We read NASA LOLA laser altimeter heights on a 240 m grid in 720 compass directions, out to 200 km.

With a flat horizon the same two points come out at 53% and 47%, nearly the same.

Lunar Landing Site Navigator5 / 7

The checks we ran on the numbers.

NASA data in the tool. The skyline comes from LOLA polar elevation grids at 240 m, and the reference tables from JPL Horizons. The 3D Moon uses the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter colour mosaic. Site coordinates come from NASA, LROC, NSSDCA and USGS pages, which the tool links to.

What it leaves out. We use terrain within 17° of a pole and a flat horizon elsewhere. We don't model solar panel angle, lander tilt or antenna pointing, and the page says so next to the numbers.

How it is built

The tool is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, and it works offline with no account. Astronomy Engine (MIT license) supplies the positions and NASA LOLA terrain supplies the skyline. The 3D Moon is WebGL code written for this project.

Use of AI

The idea is ours. We used Claude Code to write the code, the tests and these slides, and we directed and reviewed the work. Files made that way are labelled, and SI_USE.md logs each session. The project has no AI-generated images.

Next

First we would add NASA's 5 m LOLA grids for south pole landing sites, which resolve the nearest ridges. After that come solar panel tilt and an antenna model.

The live tool and the source code are at the Project link on our Space Apps page. Its “Our thinking” page explains each choice.
