    Start from a real case

    A launch slips or a site moves, and the planner needs the new lighting and radio picture the same day. Each case loads a comparison built on that kind of change.

    1. Move the site 175 km

      The IM-1 landing site against the planned IM-2 site, same day, 90 days ahead. The Earth never sets at the first. At the second it sets after 9 days, and the longest break in the radio link lasts 5 d 17 h.

    2. Land two weeks late

      The IM-1 site on its landing day and 14 days after. On the day the Sun stays above the ridges for 8 d 15 h. Two weeks on, the lander arrives in the dark and waits 12 d 2 h for sunrise.

    3. Stand on the rim of Shackleton, then on its floor

      Two places 10 km apart at the south pole, 90 days ahead. The rim has sunlight 91% of the time. The floor has none: its walls stand 20° to 24° high on every side and the polar Sun stays under 2°. Set Horizon to Flat and both places read about half lit, which is the error the terrain removes.

    4. Set the pole beside Apollo 11

      Same day, two latitudes. At the Apollo 11 site the Sun stands 86° up and the Earth sits 68° high. At the IM-1 site the Sun clears the skyline by 10.3° and the Earth stays between 2.5° and 16.4° above it, low enough for one ridge to hide either.

    Read this before trusting a number

    Which horizon the numbers use

    Within 17° of either pole the horizon is the real skyline: the height of the ground in every compass direction out to 200 km, taken from NASA's LOLA laser altimeter and seen from 2 m above the surface. The Sun or the Earth counts as up when it stands higher than the ground in its direction.

    The listed polar sites use the 240 m LOLA grid. Any other polar point uses a 960 m grid, which rounds off nearby ridges. At the IM-1 site the two skylines differ by 1.1° on average and move the first sunset by 4 hours. On a narrow peak the coarse grid can understate sunlight by a quarter of the year.

    Farther from the poles the horizon is flat, because there the Sun crosses it steeply: a ridge 2° high moves sunrise by about 4 hours. The Horizon control turns the skyline off anywhere, to show what terrain changes.

    The lander is a point with no tilt. A coordinate given to 0.01° places a site within about 300 m, and the nearest few hundred metres of ground set part of the skyline.

    How the numbers are made

    Positions come from Astronomy Engine. The Moon's orientation uses the IAU 2009 rotation model. Heights refer to the centre of the Sun or Earth disk. The Sun's disk is about 0.5° across and the Earth's about 1.9°, so each takes time to cross the skyline.

    We check the positions against NASA JPL Horizons at four places on the Moon. The largest difference found was 0.0073°, and with a flat horizon the sunset time at the IM-1 site differed by 4 seconds.

    The skyline code is tested on made-up terrain with known answers and against a second calculation written in Python; the two agree within 0.05°. The LOLA grid puts the IM-1 site 2,588 m up, where NASA gives 2,579 m, and the floor of Shackleton crater, a place in permanent shadow, gets no sunlight in the tool.

    What the energy number means

    Solar energy is the sunlight arriving on one square metre that faces the Sun the whole time: 1,361 W per m² at the Earth's distance from the Sun, scaled for the Moon's distance that day and for how much of the Sun's disk clears the skyline. A real panel delivers a share of it, set by its efficiency, its angle, and its temperature.

    Earth in view means the centre of the Earth's disk clears the skyline. It does not promise a radio link: antenna pointing, ground stations, and data rate are not modelled.

    Why the pole is different

    The Moon's axis is tilted only 1.5°, so at the poles the Sun never climbs high. It circles the horizon once a month. The Earth barely moves in the lunar sky; it rocks a few degrees up and down, which at a polar site can take it below the horizon for days.

    What the 3D Moon shows

    Imagery and elevation come from NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LROC colour mosaic, LOLA heights) through NASA's CGI Moon Kit. Slopes are drawn 3 times steeper than they are so craters show. Nothing casts a shadow on the globe, so a crater floor can look lit there while the numbers and charts, which do use the terrain, say it is dark. Near the poles the NASA mosaic also carries the shadows from the day each strip was photographed.

    The bars beside the time slider mark when the Sun is above each site's horizon. One lunar day lasts about 29.5 Earth days.
