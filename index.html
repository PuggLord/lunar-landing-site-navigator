    Start from a real case

    Launch dates slip and landing sites get moved. Each link below loads a comparison we built around a change like that. The reasons behind the tool are on a separate page, Our thinking.

    1. Move the site 175 km

      The IM-1 landing site and the planned IM-2 site, starting the same day and looking 90 days ahead. From the IM-1 site the Earth stays in view the whole time. From the IM-2 site it sets after 9 days, and the longest stretch without it is 5 d 17 h.

    2. Land two weeks late

      The IM-1 site on its landing day, and again 14 days later. A lander that arrives on the day gets 8 d 15 h before the Sun goes behind the ridges. One that arrives two weeks later lands in the dark and waits 12 d 2 h for sunrise.

    3. Compare the rim of Shackleton with its floor

      Two points about 10 km apart at the south pole, over 90 days. The rim is in sunlight 91% of the time and the floor gets none, because the crater wall is 20° to 24° high all the way round and the Sun stays under 2°. Switch Horizon to Flat to see the answer without terrain data: both points come out about half lit.

    4. Set the pole beside Apollo 11

      The IM-1 site and the Apollo 11 site on the same day. At Apollo 11 the Sun is 86° up and the Earth is at 68°. At the IM-1 site the Sun clears the skyline by 10.3°, and over the 30 days the Earth sits between 2.5° and 16.4° above it, so one ridge could block either of them.

    What the numbers assume

    The horizon

    Within 17° of either pole, the horizon is the actual skyline. We take ground heights from NASA's LOLA laser altimeter and work out how high the terrain reaches in each compass direction, out to 200 km, for a viewer 2 m above the surface. The Sun or the Earth counts as up when it is higher than the ground in its direction.

    The polar sites in the list use LOLA's 240 m grid. A point you type or click near a pole uses a coarser 960 m grid, which flattens nearby ridges. At the IM-1 site the two grids disagree by 1.1° on average and put the first sunset 4 hours apart. On a narrow peak the coarse grid can miss about a quarter of the year's sunlight.

    Away from the poles we use a flat horizon. The Sun crosses it steeply there, so a 2° ridge shifts sunrise by about 4 hours. The Horizon control switches the skyline off at any site, so you can see how much the terrain changes an answer.

    We treat the lander as a point with no tilt. A coordinate given to 0.01° is good to about 300 m, and the ground in those first few hundred metres shapes part of the skyline.

    Positions, and how we checked them

    Sun, Earth and Moon positions come from Astronomy Engine, and the Moon's orientation follows the IAU 2009 rotation model. Heights are for the centre of the disk. The Sun is about 0.5° wide and the Earth about 1.9°, so each takes a while to cross the skyline.

    We compared the positions with NASA JPL Horizons at four places on the Moon. The largest difference was 0.0073°. With a flat horizon, sunset at the IM-1 site came out 4 seconds from the Horizons time.

    We test the skyline code on made-up terrain where we know the answer, and against a second version written in Python. Those two agree within 0.05°. The LOLA grid puts the IM-1 site at 2,588 m, close to NASA's 2,579 m. The floor of Shackleton crater, which is in permanent shadow, gets no sunlight in the tool.

    Energy, and Earth in view

    Solar energy is the sunlight landing on one square metre that keeps facing the Sun. We start from 1,361 W per m² at the Earth's distance from the Sun, adjust for the Sun's distance that day, and count the part of the Sun's disk above the skyline. A real panel turns a fraction of that into electricity, depending on its efficiency and its angle.

    Earth in view means the centre of the Earth's disk is above the skyline. A working radio link also needs antenna pointing and a ground station, and we don't model those.

    Why the pole is different

    The Moon's axis is tilted by 1.5°, so at the poles the Sun stays low and circles the horizon once a month. The Earth hardly moves in the lunar sky. It drifts a few degrees up and down, and at a polar site that drift can carry it below the horizon for days.

    What the 3D Moon shows

    The imagery and heights come from NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LROC colour mosaic, LOLA heights) through NASA's CGI Moon Kit. We draw slopes 3 times steeper than they are so craters show up. The globe has no cast shadows, so a crater floor can look lit there while the charts, which use the terrain, report it dark. Near the poles the NASA mosaic also keeps the shadows from the day each strip was photographed.

    The bars next to the time slider show when the Sun is above each site's horizon. A lunar day lasts about 29.5 Earth days.
