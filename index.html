    Read this before trusting a number

    What "above the horizon" means here

    It is geometry only. The horizon is flat and the Moon is a smooth sphere of radius 1737.4 km. Hills, crater rims, lander tilt, solar panel angle, and antenna pointing are not modelled.

    Near the south pole the Sun and Earth skim the horizon, so a ridge a few degrees high can hide either one for days. Treat a height under about 5° as "check the terrain".

    Sunlight above the horizon does not promise power. Earth above the horizon does not promise a radio link.

    How the numbers are made

    Positions come from Astronomy Engine. The Moon's orientation uses the IAU 2009 rotation model. Heights refer to the centre of the Sun or Earth disk. The Sun's disk is about 0.5° across and the Earth's about 1.9°, so each takes time to cross the horizon.

    We check the calculation against NASA JPL Horizons at four places on the Moon. The largest difference found was 0.0073°, and the sunset time at the IM-1 site differed by 4 seconds.

    Why the pole is different

    The Moon's axis is tilted only 1.5°, so at the poles the Sun never climbs high. It circles the horizon once a month. The Earth barely moves in the lunar sky; it rocks a few degrees up and down, which at a polar site can take it below the horizon for days.

    What the 3D Moon shows

    Imagery and elevation come from NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LROC colour mosaic, LOLA heights) through NASA's CGI Moon Kit. Slopes are drawn 3 times steeper than they are so craters show. Nothing casts a shadow here, so a lit crater floor on the globe can be dark on the real Moon. Near the poles the NASA mosaic also carries the shadows from the day each strip was photographed.

    The bars beside the time slider mark when the Sun is above the horizon at each site. One lunar day lasts about 29.5 Earth days.
